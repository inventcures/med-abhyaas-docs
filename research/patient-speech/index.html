    Realistic patient speech for a Hindi/Hinglish voice virtual patient: research notes¶

    Scope: how real patients talk in consultations (timing, disfluency, prosody, disclosure), Indian specifics, and what Gemini Live, Google TTS and Sarvam bulbul:v3 actually let us control. Every claim carries a reference tag [Rn]. [Inference] marks my own reasoning or design advice that no source states. [Unverified] marks a widely cited number I could not check against the primary text.

    1. Summary (implementation-relevant)¶

    1. Gaps between turns are short, and a long gap carries meaning. In 10 languages the most common gap between speakers is 0 to 200 ms; the overall median is about +100 ms, and per-language medians run from 0 to +300 ms [R1]. A VP that always answers after 1 to 2 s will sound slow or evasive. One that always answers after ~0 ms will sound scripted.
    2. A delay tells the listener something. Listeners judge willingness and agreement as weaker as the silence before a reply grows from 0 to 600 to 1200 ms [R2]. Dispreferred answers become clearly more common once the gap is 700 ms or more [R3]. Use long gaps on purpose: before denials, embarrassing disclosures and "I don't know", not at random.
    3. Disfluency is normal and frequent. Spontaneous speech runs at about 6 to 10 disfluencies per 100 words, counting filled pauses, repetitions and cut-offs [R4]. Bortfeld et al. found rates rise with planning difficulty and differ by speaker role, and that fillers pattern differently from repeats and restarts [R5].
    4. "uh" and "um" are different signals. In English, "uh" announces a short delay and "um" a longer one [R6]. A filler should come before a matching silence, not replace it.
    5. Pauses cluster at clause boundaries. About two-thirds of hesitation pauses, and three-quarters of total pause time, fall between clauses [R7]. Pauses inside a phrase are rarer and come before hard lexical choices, such as a drug name, a date or a body part.
    6. Patients' opening accounts are longer than a single answer. At a Swiss tertiary outpatient clinic the uninterrupted opening statement averaged 92 s (median 59 s), and 78% of patients finished within 2 min. Talking time rose with age: 77 s for ages 17 to 29, 108 s for ages 50 to 87 [R8]. Later answers in history-taking are mostly short, but patients often "answer more than the question" with narrative expansions [R9].
    7. Indian consultations are doctor-directive, and patients hedge. In real Telugu outpatient recordings, 58 to 62% of turns were doctor-directive. Only about 32% of patient answers were confident without hedging; 59% of patient turns trailed on past the question. Doctors interrupted in 23% of turns. Patients used idioms ("head is splitting"), and only 13% of patient turns contained formal clinical terms. "Sir"-type address appeared in about 17% of turns [R10].
    8. LLM-generated patients are measurably too fluent, too certain and too deferential. Synthetic Indian dialogues were confident in 74 to 79% of answers (vs ~32% in real ones), had almost no interruption, and overused gratitude and kinship terms [R10]. This matches the user feedback and gives us concrete targets to correct.
    9. Hindi disfluency is studied but not normed. Hindi hesitations and repetitions have different predictors [R11]. Annotation schemes and synthetic-disfluency generators exist for six Indian languages [R12], and Hindi is included in DISCO [R13]. I found no published per-100-word filler rate for native Hindi conversation; use the English rates as a provisional prior.
    10. Hindi "haan" is multifunctional, and pitch carries the function. Bali (2009) shows F0 cues separate the discourse functions of hã, such as agreement versus a backchannel [R14]. A flat, TTS-default "haan" can change meaning.
    11. Pain, depression and anxiety change the voice in different directions. Experimental pain raises F0 and loudness, mainly on groan-like vowels [R15]. Depression goes with slower, quieter speech and longer or more frequent pauses [R16, R17]. Anxiety is associated with faster rate and higher pitch [R17].
    12. The provider controls are uneven. Gemini-TTS (non-Live) has [short pause] (~250 ms), [medium pause] (~500 ms), [long pause] (~1000 ms+) and [uhm]/[sigh] tags plus a natural-language style prompt [R18]. Chirp 3 HD has [pause short|long] in a markup field, speaking_rate 0.25 to 2.0 and SSML in preview (sync only), and supports hi-IN [R19]. Sarvam bulbul:v3 has only pace (0.5 to 2.0) and temperature (0.01 to 1.0); pitch and loudness are not supported on v3 [R20]. Gemini Live native audio documents no rate, pitch or pause API. Its levers are the system instruction (tone, accent, language), voice choice and VAD settings [R21, R22].
    13. Over-use is the main risk. Google warns against overusing long pauses and says unnaturally placed tags may be ignored [R18, R19]. Real disfluency is uneven: many turns are fluent, and a few are very disfluent [R5]. [Inference]

    3. Hindi/Hinglish and Indian-English specifics¶

    Sourced: - Indian outpatient interaction is directive and question-led. Patients agree readily (72%), ask few questions, hedge often, trail on past the question, and are interrupted by doctors to redirect them. Code-switching clusters around clinical concepts, and formal medical vocabulary enters only gradually [R10]. Caveat: the real data is Telugu and the Hindi data is role-played (EkaCare). - hã/haan serves several discourse functions (agreement, backchannel, continuation, and others), and F0 contour distinguishes them [R14]. - Hindi hesitations depend on lexical, syntactic and articulatory properties of both the preceding and the following word. Repetitions depend mainly on the upcoming word [R11]. [Inference] Repeat the word before a hard term ("woh... woh BP wali goli") and hesitate before low-frequency words. - Hinglish switches occur almost anywhere in the utterance. A frequent pattern is an English bare verb plus karna ("check karna", "vomit hona") [R29]. - In Indian emergency departments, clinicians routinely use non-English languages, and miscommunication incidents are common [R30]. This supports making the VP's language mixing realistic rather than fixed.

    Practitioner knowledge, not quantified by any source I found [Inference]: - Fillers and discourse markers: matlab (reformulation: "I mean"), woh (placeholder: "that... thing"), kya bolte hain / kya kehte hain (word search: "what do you call it"), haan (acknowledgement or continuation), achha (receipt), toh (turn-initial "so"), na / hai na (tag seeking agreement), jaise ("like"), bas ("that's all"), thoda (minimiser), and the plain vocalic filler aa/ā or uh. Indian English adds "actually", "basically", "what happened is", "like that only". - Deference: "doctor sahab", "sir/madam", "aap jaisa bolo". Short answers, waiting to be asked, and letting a relative answer ("inko bataiye", or the son answering for the mother). [R10] measured "sir" in ~17% of real turns. Keep it well below the synthetic rate. - Approximate numbers and time: "do-teen din se", "hafte bhar se", "kaafi time se", "Diwali ke baad se", "jab se garmi shuru hui", "ek-do baar", "sau ke upar tha shayad". Dates are anchored to events (festivals, harvest, a wedding) rather than calendar dates. Ranges with -teen/-char ("teen-char") are idiomatic. - Indirect symptom terms: "chakkar aana" (anything from vertigo to presyncope), "ghabrahat" (anxiety, palpitations or dyspnoea), "kamzori", "gas banna" (often chest or epigastric pain), "sir bhaari", "dil baith raha hai", "neeche ki taraf" (genital and urinary euphemism). - Minimising and hedging: "thoda sa", "zyada nahi", "kabhi kabhi", "pata nahi doctor sahab", "aisa lagta hai".

    4. How patients answer¶

    • Adjacency: Most history-taking answers are type-conforming and short. Patients still regularly expand with narrative to pursue their own aims (worry, an explanation, legitimacy) [R9]. Heritage & Robinson show patients justify the visit ("doctorability") and treat current symptoms as the point where the doctor takes over [R31, R32].
    • Answering a different question: It happens when the question touches a worry. Real Indian patients trail past the question in 59% of turns [R10].
    • Self-correction: Repairs and restarts track planning load [R5]. Typical triggers are numbers, dates, drug names and sequences of events. [Inference]
    • Uncertainty: Hedged answers outnumber confident ones roughly 2:1 in real Indian data [R10]. Real patients say "I don't know" or "not sure" with a delay and a dispreferred format [R3].
    • Emotion: Pain raises pitch and loudness [R15]. Low mood slows and quietens speech [R16]. Native-audio models can adapt to the user's emotional expression ("affective dialog") on supported 2.5 models [R22].

    5. Design recommendations¶

    A. Generate the disfluency in the LLM text, from a per-turn plan. [Inference throughout, grounded in the sources cited] 1. Per-turn "speech plan" before generation. Sample: latency_ms, disfluency_budget (0 to 3 events), answer_shape (minimal / minimal+expansion / narrative / off-target / don't-know), certainty (confident / hedged / unsure). Condition on case state: question difficulty, sensitivity, recall load, persona age, pain and mood. 2. Distribution targets for an outpatient persona: - About 40 to 50% of turns fully fluent. - Overall rate about 4 to 8 disfluent events per 100 words, using [R4, R5] as the prior. - Hedged ≥ confident [R10]. - Narrative expansion in a minority of turns, but present. - Answer-only turns kept short (1 to 2 clauses). 3. Placement rules: - Put fillers turn-initially, at clause boundaries, and before a number, date, drug name, body part or embarrassing word [R7, R11]. - Repeat the word before a hard term ("woh... woh goli"). - Repair numbers ("teen... nahi, chaar din"). - Allow a trailing-off ending ("aur phir... pata nahi") and a restart with a new train of thought ("matlab pehle... nahi, pehle bukhar aaya tha"). 4. Latency policy, used as silence before audio: - Simple yes/no or name: 150 to 400 ms. - Ordinary history item: 300 to 800 ms. - Recall (dates, drugs, sequences): 800 to 1500 ms, often with a filler first. - Sensitive or dispreferred (alcohol, sexual history, non-adherence, "no"): 700 to 2000 ms [R2, R3]. - Add age and depression multipliers (slower, more pauses) [R8, R16, R26]. 5. Hard guardrails: - Disfluency must never change the facts. A repair must end on the case-correct value. - Never put a filler next to a negation (nahi, na, mat) in a way that makes the polarity ambiguous. - Never use na as a tag immediately after a negative clause. - A [pause] or filler must not split a number ("do... sau") unless the case value is "200". - Validate the final string against the case facts after disfluency insertion, not before. 6. Avoid caricature: - No more than one stacked filler. - Do not use the same marker twice in a turn. - Rotate markers across turns. - Keep "doctor sahab" and "sir" rare (target ≤ ~1 in 5 turns [R10]). - Avoid uniform stammer; real disfluency is bursty [R5]. - Avoid a filler in every turn. - Fully fluent turns must remain common.

For a real silence, split the text into segments and insert silence client-side between chunks. [Inference]

    C. Per-turn variation: Draw pace per clause segment rather than per turn: about 0.85 to 1.1 for a calm adult, lower for depressed or elderly personas. Keep bulbul temperature around 0.5 to 0.75 to avoid artifacts [R20]. Apply jitter of about ±10% within a turn and drift slowly across the session as fatigue sets in. [Inference]

    6. Evaluation¶

    1. Objective statistics vs reference. For each VP session, compute:
    2. pre-answer latency distribution;
    3. within-turn silent-pause count and duration (≥200 ms);
    4. fillers and repairs per 100 words;
    5. percentage of fluent turns;
    6. percentage of hedged answers;
    7. answer length in words.

    Compare against [R1] (gaps), [R4, R5] (disfluency), [R10] (hedging, trailing, address terms) and an in-house sample of real Hindi OPD audio with consent. Flag runs that fall outside the target bands. 2. Listener ratings. Run a blind MOS-style naturalness rating ("human vs VP" with a 5-point realism scale), rated by Indian clinicians and trainees. Compare baseline, disfluency-on and over-disfluent conditions to find the caricature threshold. Ask raters separately about fact clarity, so realism does not cost the student information. 3. Turing-style discrimination. A small study found psychotherapy trainees could not tell trained SPs from real patients [R34]. Use the same design with VP clips against real or SP clips. 4. Portrayal standards. The ASPE Standards of Best Practice have a domain for SP training in role portrayal: consistency, accuracy, and realistic affect [R35]. Use it for a faculty review checklist. 5. Corpora: - Switchboard (English disfluency annotation) [R36]. - PriMock57: 57 mock UK primary-care consultations with audio and transcripts [R37]. - IndicVoices: 22 languages, about 74% extempore and 17% conversational in v1; CC BY 4.0 [R38]. - DISCO: Hindi disfluency correction [R13]. - IIIT-H Indian-language disfluency annotation [R12]. - Hindi-English code-switching speech corpus [R39]. - Real Telugu OPD (PrivTel) is proprietary [R10].
